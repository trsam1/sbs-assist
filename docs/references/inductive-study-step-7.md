Step 7—Analyze the Structure of the Book

Overview

Having observed the text as a scroll (Step 4), defined the key words (Step 5), and identified the
antecedents, referents, audience, and speaker (Step 6), the student is now ready to step back and
look at the book as a whole. Step 7 asks: how is this book put together? What kind of literature is
it, what is it driving at, where does it begin and end its thought, and into what sections does it
naturally fall? Working out a book's structure is one of the most rewarding steps of observation,
because it shows the author's own flow of thought and keeps interpretation anchored to the book's
design rather than to isolated verses.

Much of this work is mechanical, and this is where careful observation pays off. Repeated words can
be counted. Transitional statements, direct address, strong declaratives, and commands can be
located. The sections they mark can be written down. The goal of the step is not for a tool to
decide the structure, but for the student to see the signals clearly and draw the divisions
themselves.

Genre

Before analyzing a book's structure, identify its genre—the kind of literature it is—because
different genres are structured differently and are read differently. Most of the Bible is one of a
handful of genres:

- Historical narrative. The author tells a story about real people, places, and events, usually
  (though not always strictly) in chronological order. Genesis, the Books of Samuel, Ruth, and Acts
  are narrative. Narrative is structured by scenes and episodes.
- Law. Legal and covenant material, as in large portions of Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and
  Deuteronomy. It is structured by collections of commands and case law.
- Poetry. The Psalms are poetry—the lyrics of Jewish songs—and Lamentations is a collection of
  poems. Hebrew poetry is structured by parallelism and stanza, not by narrative sequence.
- Wisdom. Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Job weigh how life works under God. Proverbs is structured by
  collections of sayings; Job and Ecclesiastes are framed by a narrative or a reflective argument.
- Prophecy. The prophets deliver God's word to a people, often in oracles. Structure follows the
  oracles and their occasions.
- Letters (epistles). Much of the New Testament is first-person letters written by apostles and
  early leaders to the first Christians. A letter typically has an opening (sender, recipients,
  greeting), a body (often doctrine followed by application), and a closing (greetings, benediction).
- Apocalyptic literature. Daniel and Revelation use vivid symbolic visions. Structure follows the
  sequence of visions.

Other genres found in Scripture include musical lyric, allegory, parable, genealogy, proverb,
prayer, and biography. A single book may contain more than one genre; naming the dominant genre, and
noting where others appear, is part of analyzing the structure.

Thesis

Many books state, or strongly imply, a central purpose—a thesis. The Apostle John states his
plainly near the end of his Gospel: "but these have been written so that you may believe that Jesus
is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing you may have life in His name" (John 20:31). Luke
opens his Gospel with his purpose, "so that you may know the exact truth about the things you have
been taught" (Luke 1:4). When a book states its purpose, that statement governs how its sections
hang together. When it does not state one outright, the repeated words, the recurring themes, and
the structure itself point to it.

Reading for Structural Signals

A book's structure is not usually announced in a table of contents; it is signaled in the text. The
student reads the whole book looking for the signals below and marks where they occur.

Repeated words and phrases. Authors repeat what matters to them. A word or phrase that recurs far
more often than ordinary language would require is almost always a focus word of the book, and the
places it clusters often mark the book's sections. Leviticus repeats "holy" and "clean/unclean";
the command "Speak to the sons of Israel" occurs fifteen times. In 1 Corinthians Paul repeats "now
concerning" to introduce each new subject the Corinthians had written to him about. Counting the
repeated words of a book—and noticing where they cluster—is one of the most reliable ways into its
structure. (Count content words; ordinary function words like "the" and "and" repeat for reasons of
grammar, not emphasis.)

Transitional statements. Authors mark the seams of their thought with transitions. Watch for phrases
such as "now concerning," "finally," "therefore," "but now," "after these things," "in those days,"
and "then." A transition usually signals the end of one section and the start of another.

Strong declarative statements. A forceful, summarizing declaration often sits at a structural hinge
—the thesis of a section or of the book. Paul's "I am not ashamed of the gospel" (Romans 1:16) opens
the argument of Romans; "Vanity of vanities! All is vanity" (Ecclesiastes 1:2) sets the theme of
Ecclesiastes.

Commands. Imperatives frequently mark the application portion of a letter or the covenant demands of
a legal section. The movement from statement to command (indicative to imperative) is itself a
structural signal—many letters set out doctrine first and then turn to the commands that follow from
it.

Direct address. When an author turns to speak directly to the reader—"my brethren," "beloved,"
"brethren," "O Timothy," "you yourselves know"—he is usually opening or closing a section, or driving
a point home. The vocative is a reliable marker of a seam.

The structure of similar books. A book is rarely alone in its genre. The letters share a common
shape (opening, body, closing); the Gospels share a common arc; the narratives share scene-based
telling. Comparing a book with others of its kind helps the student see where its own sections begin
and end, and where it departs from the pattern.

Tone. The emotional register of a book—its tone—is itself an observation worth recording, and shifts
in tone often mark structural shifts. Paul's tone toward the Galatians is urgent and alarmed; toward
the Philippians it is warm and thankful. Lamentations grieves throughout; Philippians rejoices. A
change in tone frequently coincides with a change of section.

Introduction and Conclusion

Pay particular attention to how a book opens and closes. Authors use introductions to set the stage
—to name the audience, state the purpose, strike the keynote—and conclusions to summarize, exhort,
and sign off. In the letters especially, the opening (sender, recipients, greeting, often a
thanksgiving) and the closing (final greetings, benediction) are clearly bounded and easy to mark.
Identifying the introduction and conclusion first often makes the body's own sections easier to see.

The Elements Chart

A useful tool for comparing structure is an elements chart: a simple grid that records which
structural elements occur where in the book. Down one side the student lists the elements observed—
for a letter, that might be greeting, thanksgiving, doctrine, exhortation, personal news,
benediction; for a narrative, the scenes or episodes. Across the top the student lists the sections
or passages. Marking which element occurs in which section lays the book's shape out visually and
makes gaps and repetitions obvious. The chart is the student's own; its value is in forcing a careful
reading, not in any fixed set of elements.

A Caution: The Divisions Are Uninspired

The chapter and verse numbers in our Bibles, and even many paragraph breaks, were added long after
the books were written. They are convenient for reference, but they are not inspired and are
sometimes placed where they interrupt rather than reflect the author's flow of thought. A section
can begin in the middle of a chapter; a sentence can run across a chapter break. When analyzing
structure, the student must read the text itself and let the author's own signals—his repeated
words, transitions, declaratives, commands, direct address, and shifts of tone—set the divisions,
verifying the printed divisions against the text rather than trusting them.

Making the Analysis

Working through Step 7, the student:

1. Names the book's genre (and any secondary genres).
2. States, or works out, the book's thesis or purpose.
3. Reads the whole book, marking the structural signals—repeated words, transitional statements,
   strong declaratives, commands, direct address—and where they occur.
4. Notes the book's tone and any shifts in it.
5. Identifies the introduction and conclusion.
6. Draws the book into its sections, letting the signals set the divisions and treating the printed
   chapter/verse divisions as unverified.
7. Optionally lays the elements out in a chart to compare the structure against similar books.

The result is a clear picture of how the book is put together—its genre, its thesis, and its
sections—which becomes the foundation for Step 8, Make a Provisional Outline.
