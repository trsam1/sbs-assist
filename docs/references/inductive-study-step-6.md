Pronouns
Pronouns are used in place of nouns and function in the same way. The noun being replaced is
called the pronoun’s antecedent. For example, Matthew writes, “Now when Jesus heard this, He
marveled, and said to those who were following, ‘Truly I say to you, I have not found such great
faith with anyone in Israel’ ” (Matthew 8:10). The pronoun “He” replaces the proper noun “Jesus.”
This makes “Jesus” the antecedent of “He.”
Like nouns, pronouns have gender, number, and case. They also possess the quality of person, which
indicates the person to whom the pronoun refers. Pronouns are in one of three persons: first,
second, or third.

54Identifying Antecedents
When encountering pronouns and possessive adjectives in Scripture, we should always pause and
identify the antecedent—that is, the person or thing to which the pronoun refers. This is especially
important when the pronouns in a passage refer to more than one Person of the Trinity.
Usually the antecedents of the pronouns in a passage are evident. For example, consider the
passage below from Exodus. It refers to Moses’ infancy. Though the text contains 27 pronouns
(italicized) and 5 possessive adjectives (underlined), the antecedent of each is apparent:
Then the daughter of Pharaoh came down to bathe at the Nile, with her maidens
walking alongside the Nile; and she saw the basket among the reeds and sent her maid,
and she brought it to her. When she opened it, she saw the child, and behold, the boy
was crying. And she had pity on him and said, “This is one of the Hebrews’ children.”
Then his sister said to Pharaoh’s daughter, “Shall I go and call a nurse for you from
the Hebrew women, that she may nurse the child for you?” And Pharaoh’s daughter
said to her, “Go ahead.” So the girl went and called the child’s mother. Then
Pharaoh’s daughter said to her, “Take this child away and nurse him for me and I shall
give you your wages.” So the woman took the child and nursed him. And the child
grew, and she brought him to Pharaoh’s daughter, and he became her son. And she
named him Moses, and said, “Because I drew him out of the water. (Exodus 2:5-10)
In more theologically complex passages, the antecedents of pronouns and possessive adjectives may
be more difficult to determine. Consider the following three verses. Can you identify the
antecedents of its fifteen pronouns (italicized) and three possessive adjectives (underlined)?
And when you were dead in your transgressions and the uncircumcision of your flesh,
He made you alive together with Him, having forgiven us all our transgressions, having
canceled out the certificate of debt consisting of decrees against us and which was

He had disarmed the rulers and authorities, He made a public display of them, having
triumphed over them through Him. (Colossians 2:13-15)
Identifying the antecedents in this passage requires knowledge of the greater context of the verse,
though some can be determined through the process of elimination. Annotating the text with the

uncircumcision of your [Colossians] flesh, He [Father] made you [Colossians] alive
together with Him [Jesus], having forgiven us [Paul & Colossians] all our [Paul &
Colossians] transgressions, having canceled out the certificate of debt consisting of
decrees against us [Paul & Colossians] and which was hostile to us [Paul &

55Referent
Much like pronouns, other terms can serve in place of a person or thing. The person or thing to
which the term refers is called the referent. For example, consider the following text:
Jesus answered and said, “This voice has not come for My sake, but for your sakes.
Now judgment is upon this world; now the ruler of this world shall be cast
out.” (John 12:30-31)
Here, the referent of “the ruler of this world” is Satan. The referent of “this world” is the people
of this world and the world’s way of doing things.
Audience
Before you can correctly interpret a book of the Bible, you must identify the target audience. Was
the prophet writing to Jews, Gentiles, Jews and Gentiles, believers, nonbelievers, or all people? If
the prophet was writing to a Jewish audience, what he says does not necessarily apply to a Gentile
audience. If the prophet was writing to a Christian audience, what he says does not necessarily apply
to non-Christians.
Paul’s First Letter to the Corinthians is an example of what can happen when the intended audience
is misunderstood. In a portion of his earlier letter to the Corinthians, they thought he was writing
about unbelievers, when in fact he was writing about believers. In 1 Corinthians, Paul corrects this
misunderstanding:
I wrote you in my letter not to associate with immoral people; I did not at all mean
with the immoral people of this world, or with the covetous and swindlers, or with
idolaters; for then you would have to go out of the world. But actually, I wrote to you
not to associate with any so-called brother if he should be an immoral person, or
covetous, or an idolater, or a reviler, or a drunkard, or a swindler—not even to eat
with such a one. For what have I to do with judging outsiders? Do you not judge
those who are within the church? But those who are outside, God judges. Remove
the wicked man from among yourselves. (1 Corinthians 5:9-13)
Often an author will identify his audience in the opening verses of a book. For example, Leviticus
begins: “Then the Lord called to Moses and spoke to him from the tent of meeting, saying, ‘Speak
to the sons of Israel and say to them…” (Leviticus 1:1-2). Clearly, the Jewish people are the
intended audience. The command “Speak to the sons of Israel” occurs 15 times in the book.
Likewise, Peter identifies his audience in the opening verses of his letter, writing, “Peter, an apostle
of Jesus Christ, to those who reside as aliens, scattered throughout Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia,
Asia, and Bithynia…” (1 Peter 1:1).
Though the intended readership of some books is less clear, no book is without some indication of
the intended audience. A distinctive feature of the Gospel of Mark, for example, is that the author
explains five Aramaic statements. This indicates that Gentiles were likely the intended audience,
56since most Jews spoke Aramaic as their first language. Of course, it is also possible that a mixed
readership was intended.
Point of View
Most of the Bible is written as historical narrative. In this genre, the author tells a story. The author
relates factual information about real people, places, and events. The story is usually organized
chronologically, but not necessarily strictly chronologically. Examples of historical narrative include
the story of the Flood (Genesis 6:1-9:29), the Book of Ruth, the Books of 1 and 2 Samuel, and the
Book of Acts. Historical narrative can be written from various point-of-view perspectives.
FIRST-PERSON POINT OF VIEW
In first-person point of view, the narrator is a character in the story. He presents the events that
occurred from his perspective. Nehemiah, for example, uses the first-person point of view in telling
his story as the cupbearer of Artaxerxes I, King of Persia (reigned 465-424 BC). He writes, “Now it
happened in the month Chislev, in the twentieth year, while I was in Susa the capitol, that Hanani,
one of my brothers, and some men from Judah came; and I asked them concerning the Jews who
had escaped and had survived the captivity, and about Jerusalem” (Nehemiah 1:1-2). Notice the use
of the first-person, singular, personal pronoun I in this text. It is a marker of the first-person point
of view. We find the same in the Apostle John’s narrative in the Book of Revelation. He writes, “I
was in the Spirit on the Lord's Day, and I heard behind me a loud voice like the sound of a trumpet,
saying, ‘Write in a book what you see, and send it to the seven churches: to Ephesus and to Smyrna
and to Pergamum and to Thyatira and to Sardis and to Philadelphia and to Laodicea.’ And I turned
to see the voice that was speaking with me. And having turned I saw seven golden
lampstands” (Revelation 1:10-12). John proceeds to give us a first-person account of what he saw,
what he experienced, and what was revealed to him.
SECOND-PERSON POINT OF VIEW
In second-person point of view, the narrator uses the second-person personal pronoun you. In some
genres, the use of the second-person point of view is directed to the reader, making the reader feel
like a character in the story. However, unless you are writing a letter or giving instructions to the
reader, this style is difficult to maintain and can be very limiting. For this reason, it is rarely used in
literature and is not used by any of the authors of the Bible.
57THIRD-PERSON POINT OF VIEW
In third-person point of view, the narrator tells the story from the perspective of one of its
characters. We see and experience what the character sees and experiences. This is communicated by
the use of third-person personal pronouns: he, she, it, they. The Book of Jonah is told through the
third-person perspective of Jonah, the central character of the story. The reader follows Jonah from
Israel to his escape by sea, into the belly of the great fish, onto dry land, to the city of Nineveh, and
finally to the outskirts of the city to sit and stew under a blazing hot sun. The author writes, “Then
Jonah went out from the city and sat east of it. There he made a shelter for himself and sat under it
in the shade until he could see what would happen in the city” (Jonah 4:5). Though the book is
generally believed to have been written by Jonah, the author never explicitly states this. Some books
written from a third-person point of view begin with the perspective of one character in the story
and then change to the perspective of another character. The perspective can even change several
times throughout a story.
AUTHORIAL POINT OF VIEW
In the authorial point of view, the narrator speaks as the author, retelling the story from his
perspective and with seemingly full knowledge of the events and related circumstances. The author
knows what all characters say, their motivations, and what everyone in the story is doing with almost
God-like knowledge. For this reason, this perspective is also called the authorial omniscient point of
view.
The authorial point of view is the most common storytelling point of view. It is similar to third-
person point of view, since the author writes about what others do, say, and experience. The author
uses third-person personal pronouns: he, she, it, they. However, the authorial point of view differs
from third-person point of view, because the author does not limit himself to the perspective of
one person and speaks authoritatively about the story as if he had nearly perfect knowledge of all
events. The Gospel of Matthew is written this way. Matthew reports the circumstances surrounding
Jesus’ birth, John’s Baptism, Jesus’ temptation in the wilderness, the Lord’s teaching and ministry,
and His death and resurrection. Matthew also knows about the Pharisees’ conspiratorial plotting
(Matthew 22:15-16); the deliberations of the Sanhedrin, the Jewish supreme council (Matthew
26:57-68); Peter’s denials (Matthew 26:69-75); Pilate being warned by his wife (Matthew 27:19); the
chief priests’ request to Pilate that guards be placed at Jesus’ tomb (Matthew 27:62-66); the reaction
of the guards to Jesus’ resurrection (Matthew 28:2-4); and the guards reporting to the chief priest
that Jesus had risen (Matthew 28:11-15). Information about these events was most likely obtained
from individuals present at the time who later became believers and members of the church in
Jerusalem.
58MULTIPLE POINTS OF VIEW
Sometimes an author will change the point of view during the story. We see this, for example, in the
Book of Ezra, which was written by Ezra. In the first six chapters, Ezra tells the story of
Zerubbabel’s rebuilding of the temple in Jerusalem. Ezra uses the authorial point of view, or
omniscient point of view. In chapters seven through ten, Ezra tells the story of his own work in
Jerusalem, building up the Jewish people. In this section of the book, he is the main character, yet
he continues in the authorial point of view. For example, he writes, “This Ezra went up from
Babylon, and he was a scribe skilled in the Law of Moses, which the Lord God of Israel had given;
and the king granted him all he requested because the hand of the Lord his God was upon
him” (Ezra 7:6). Occasionally, Ezra shifts to the first-person point of view. He writes, for example:
“Thus I was strengthened according to the hand of the Lord my God upon me, and I gathered
leading men from Israel to go up with me” (Ezra 7:28).
Daniel makes similar changes in point of view throughout his book. In Daniel 1:8, he writes, “But
Daniel made up his mind that he would not defile himself with the king’s choice food,” using the
third-person pronoun. Later, he uses the first-person pronoun: “And I looked in the vision, and it
came about while I was looking, that I was in the citadel of Susa, which is in the province of Elam;
and I looked in the vision, and I myself was beside the Ulai Canal” (Daniel 8:2).
Luke does the same in the Book of Acts. In the early chapters, he uses the authorial point of view,
mainly using the third-person plural personal pronoun they: “Then they returned to Jerusalem from
the mount called Olivet, which is near Jerusalem, a Sabbath day's journey away. And when they had
entered, they went up to the upper room, where they were staying” (Acts 1:12-13). Notice the
change in pronouns, however, from they to we in Acts 16:
And they passed through the Phrygian and Galatian region, having been forbidden by
the Holy Spirit to speak the word in Asia; and when they had come to Mysia, they were
trying to go into Bithynia, and the Spirit of Jesus did not permit them; and passing by
Mysia, they came down to Troas. And a vision appeared to Paul in the night: a certain
man of Macedonia was standing and appealing to him, and saying, “Come over to
Macedonia and help us.” And when he had seen the vision, immediately we sought to
go into Macedonia, concluding that God had called us to preach the gospel to them.
Therefore putting out to sea from Troas, we ran a straight course to Samothrace, and
on the day following to Neapolis; and from there to Philippi, which is a leading city
of the district of Macedonia, a Roman colony; and we were staying in this city for
some days. (Acts 16:6-12 (emphasis added))
Apparently, Luke joined Paul on his journey to Macedonia. He communicates this by moving from
the third-person plural pronoun they to the first-person plural pronoun we. Luke sails with Paul from
Troas to Samothrace, Neapolis, and finally Philippi. He is with Paul when he meets Lydia (Acts
16:11-15). When Paul and Silas are thrown into prison a short time later, Luke is not with them
(Acts 16:16-34). Upon their release, Paul and Silas sail west to Thessalonica, apparently without

Luke rejoins Paul in Troas. Their reunion is expressed by a switch to the first-person plural
pronoun, writing, “And we sailed from Philippi after the days of Unleavened Bread, and came to
them at Troas within five days; and there we stayed seven days. And on the first day of the week,
when we were gathered together to break bread, Paul began talking to them, intending to depart the
next day, and he prolonged his message until midnight” (Acts 20:6-7). Luke continues with Paul for
the remainder of his travels recorded in the Book of Acts.
Not all of the Bible is historical narrative. The Psalms are poetry, the lyrics of Jewish songs.
Lamentations, as the title indicates, is a collection of five poems lamenting the destruction of
Jerusalem and the Jewish temple by the Babylonians in 586 BC. Much of the New Testament is
letters written by various apostles and early leaders to the first Christians. These are first-person
exhortations and teaching letters in which the author addresses his readers directly. Paul, for
example, states in the opening of his Letter to the Romans, “I thank my God through Jesus Christ
for you all…” (Romans 1:8). In this letter, Paul uses the personal pronoun I 118 times. In his First
Letter to the Corinthians, he uses I 124 times. Other biblical genres include musical lyric, allegory,
parable, genealogy, proverb, prayer, biography, and apocalyptic literature.

through the writing. Often, the author and the speaker are the same person. For example, David
writes:
O Lord, our Lord,
How majestic is Thy name in all the earth,
Who hast displayed Thy splendor above the heavens! (Psalm 8:1)

61A surprisingly large amount of Scripture also includes direct quotation and dialogue. These portions
also involve a change of speaker. For example, in the first three chapters of the Book of Genesis,
we read the words of Moses the narrator, God, Adam, Eve, and Satan. To interpret these sections
correctly, we must keep track of who says what.
When reading the Bible, it is important to identify the speaker, because not everything in the Bible
is true in an absolute sense. For example, Satan told Eve, “You surely shall not die!” (Genesis 3:4).
What Satan said was not true, for when they ate the forbidden fruit, they died. Virtually everything
Satan says in the Bible is untrue. “He is a liar, and the father of lies” (John 8:44).
Not everything in the Book of Ecclesiastes is absolutely true. Solomon is the author and the
speaker. He writes as a man sees life “under the sun,” a term he uses 29 times. “Vanity of vanities!
All is vanity” (Ecclesiastes 1:2), Solomon decries in the opening words of his book. Much of what
follows is just that—vanity. It is the empty philosophy of a foolish king.
Much of the counsel of Job’s four friends is also suspect. What they said is recorded in the Bible,
but what they said was not true. God points this out in Job 38:1-41:34.
The imprecatory Psalms are another example where identification of the speaker is essential to our
understanding. For example, consider the following:
O daughter of Babylon, you devastated one,
How blessed will be the one who repays you
With the recompense with which you have repaid us.
How blessed will be the one who seizes and dashes your little ones
Against the rock. (Psalm 137:8-9)
Are we to conclude from this that God sanctions cruelty to children? Not at all. The speaker is not
God or God’s prophet. The speakers are unbelieving Jews, who are under the judgment of God. He
has sent them into exile in Babylon. There, rather than repenting of their sins, they cry out to God
to judge their enemies. They express their hatred for the Babylonians through the violent acts that
they refer to in this passage.
